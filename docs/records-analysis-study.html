Four ways to read the record

Every mark this app records carries a letter, a word, an āyah and a printed page. The analysis screen keeps the count and throws the location away, then reports an average score across judge sections that were never comparable. Four candidates that each start from a question somebody actually asked, and one of them puts the Mushaf page back at the centre of the evidence.

01

The problem

A Mistake carries tid, wordText, surah, ayah, page, glyph, label and amount — a letter, located on a printed page, inside the word it belongs to. computeRecords() reduces all of it to four category counts, a top-ten letter frequency and a top-eight repeated-location list. Page is discarded. Word is discarded. What remains is a bar chart any plus-minus judging app could draw, on the one screen where the app's own differentiator was supposed to pay off.

Above it sit three metric cards. Average score is the largest number on the screen, and it is the mean of total / totalMax across every session in scope. Sessions are judge sections: a Jalī-only assignment and a full-panel assignment have different maxima and different rubrics, and the default filter is All judges · All sections. The product foundation states the rule this brushes against — do not rank across categories with different rubrics — and while a metric card does not rank, it invites the same reading, in 26px type.

The accountability layer is the part that is simply missing. The vision document promises a record that “exposes weak judges”. Judge appears in this screen as a dropdown and as a name in a row. There is no view in which one judge's marking can be compared with another's — candidate C is that view, and most of its design work turns out to be refusing to draw a comparison that the data cannot support.

All four candidates read the same invented record: 14 reciters, 3 judges, 2 divisions, 28 marks landing on Sūrat al-Falaq. The hottest word is 113:4 — the one the foundation document already singles out as the letter-boundary problem case. The counters under each candidate are measured from the DOM at run time.

Four candidates

AThe page as the evidenceDensity on the field of play

The Mushaf page is the chart. Every word a reciter was marked on carries a wash whose weight is how many reciters were marked there, so the shape of the difficulty is read off the page rather than off a bar. Baseball and football analytics settled long ago that a spatial problem is read spatially; here the field of play is the page the app already draws better than anything else it owns.

BThe teaching listRunning records

Every row is a claim a teacher can act on rather than a count: the word, where it sits, how many reciters of how many, and which criterion it mostly fell under. Marie Clay's running records and Goodman's miscue analysis have coded oral reading errors onto the text since the sixties for exactly this purpose — the analysis ends in what to teach next. Rows too thin to be a pattern are held back rather than ranked.

CJudge calibrationHawk–dove, FIG JEP

The accountability screen the vision promised. Medical examining boards have measured examiner leniency for twenty years — in one MRCP(UK) study, roughly 12% of score variance sat in examiner stringency rather than in the candidates — and gymnastics runs a whole Judge Evaluation Program on the same principle. Both work because assessors overlap. So the hard part of this screen is not the chart; it is refusing to draw one where no two judges have scored the same reciter.

Descriptive, not a verdict. Deduction rates are shaped by who each judge was given as much as by how strictly they mark. A deviation is only shown for criteria where two judges scored the same reciters, and the overlap is stated on the row.

03

How they compare

Clicks to the worst word is the benchmark task: starting from a cold screen, reach the fact that nine of fourteen reciters were marked on 113:4. Questions answered on the first screen counts, out of the five below, how many a candidate answers without a further click. Numbers nobody asked for counts figures on screen that answer none of them — today's screen opens with three.

The five: 1. Which mistakes repeat across reciters? 2. Where on the page do they cluster? 3. Is any judge marking differently from the others? 4. How is one school or division doing? 5. Where is that reciter's result from Tuesday?

04

Against the grammar

Candidate A needs amendment 1 and amendment 3 before it can ship; B and D need neither. All four drop the average-score card, which is not a candidate variable — it is the arithmetic in §01.

A — The page as the evidence

Keeps

  • Rule 1, under amendment 3 — the page is the evidence here too, so analysis gets the room and the filters are chrome down the side.
  • Rule 2 — one word, one place to press. Pressing it is what opens the evidence panel; nothing else on screen claims that job.
  • Rule 9 — density is carried by an underline weight as well as a wash, so it survives a projector and a colour-blind reader.

Bends

  • Rule 3, colour is a verdict — a heat overlay is frequency, not a verdict, and painting it in Jalī red would be a false signal. Bent under amendment 1, and the prototype demonstrates the amendment: with All selected the page is ink alpha only; choose one criterion and the wash takes that criterion's colour, at which point it is reporting a verdict that was actually given. Press the filters and watch it happen.
  • Rule 8 — the page runs at 27px, off the type scale, because it is the Mushaf's own scale rather than the interface's. The app already grants this everywhere else it draws the page.

B — The teaching list

Keeps

  • Rule 6 — a row that reads “9 of 14 reciters, mostly Jalī” needs no chart title and no legend explaining what a bar means.
  • Rule 9 — every count is stated against its denominator, so a row can never quietly imply a rate it does not have.
  • Rule 3 — no colour at all; the criterion is a word.

Bends

  • Nothing in the grammar. Its cost is elsewhere: it needs a rule the grammar does not yet have about when a count is too thin to display as a pattern. The prototype holds rows under three reciters behind a gate; that threshold is a product decision, not a design one, and it wants an owner.

C — Judge calibration

Keeps

  • Rule 6 — the empty state is a sentence saying why there is nothing to compare, which is the whole content of that row.
  • Rule 9 — every deviation carries its overlap count in the same row, so the number can never be read without its sample.

Bends

  • Rule 1 — this screen has no evidence on it at all, only summaries of evidence. Bent knowingly: a judge's marking pattern has no single page to point at, which is also why it should sit beside candidate A rather than replace it.
  • It reaches past design into statistics. Anything past a mean deviation on shared reciters needs the Rasch-style treatment named in the plan, and that is a modelling decision with a rules owner, not a screen.

D — Ask one question

Keeps

  • Rule 6, in its strongest form — there is no sentence on screen that the controls already show, because there is almost nothing on screen.
  • Rule 10 — the questions are 44px rows, and this is the only candidate that keeps the live-judging target size without paying for it in density.

Bends

  • Rule 1 — the first screen is entirely chrome. Every pixel is a question and none is evidence, which is the exact inversion of the rule.
  • It works only if the question list is right. A question nobody thought to add is a question the screen cannot answer, and the current screen at least lets you stumble on something.
