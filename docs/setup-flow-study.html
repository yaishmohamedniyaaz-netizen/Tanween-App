The setup screen

Before a single reciter is heard, someone has to prepare eight tasks and press Start. Today that is a two-pane screen carrying 248 words, and it does not fit a laptop. Four ways of laying it out follow, each built as a working prototype of the whole screen rather than a picture of one. No candidate is picked here.

01

The problem

The owner's words for this screen are “overwhelming, text heavy and clunky compared to the Mushaf view”. The Mushaf view is the one place in the app where the evidence gets the room and the chrome gets out of the way. The setup screen is the opposite: two panes, both of them talking.

248 words on one screen to convey nine facts. The nine are the competition name, its edition, the division count, the participant count, the panel size, which criteria that panel covers, the marks total, the question rules, and the draft count. Nine facts, 248 words — roughly 27 words per fact, on a screen whose entire job is to let someone check nine things and press a button. Rule 1 fails here in its own words: the explanation is larger than the thing being explained.

The screen never quite fits. It overflows the viewport by 69px at 1024×768, by 37px at 1280×800, and by 15px even at 1400×900. The cause is structural rather than incidental: the shell is min-height: 100vh and the header above it is sticky, so the content is handed a full viewport of height inside a viewport that has already spent some. A judge scrolls a settings screen that should fit.

Every task states itself twice. The left rail gives each task a title and then a subtitle that restates the title: “Competition details / Name and edition”, “Participants / Import and verify the roster”, “Draft questions / Build and preview passages”. Eight titles, eight subtitles, eight status words. Rule 6 says to delete every sentence that repeats what the controls already show; the rail is sixteen lines of the same eight facts.

The right pane repeats the trick at a larger scale. Every review row carries a label, a value, a long explanatory sentence and a Change link — and two of those sentences are the same criterion list printed twice, once under Judging panel and once under Marks. The app writes that criterion as Adu / Raagu, with the slash, and it appears on this screen four times.

Every candidate below renders the same competition: four divisions, eight participants, one judge, 100 marks, manual questions at seven lines, no drafts. Seven tasks are complete; Draft questions is optional and sits at 0. Each reaches the same “Start sample” button and carries the same freeze note — thirteen words that no candidate can save, on whichever screen offers Start.

02

Four candidates

Each card is a live prototype of the whole screen, not a fragment. Press things: A selects a task in its rail, B expands a line in place, C steps forward and back, D edits a value where it is read. Reset restores the card. The caption under each prototype is measured at run time with getBoundingClientRect() and by reading the rendered text.

How the words are counted. The count is taken from the prototype's rendered text only — the card's caption, its measurement line and its action counter are outside the measured region. Text that is collapsed, on another step, or otherwise not rendered does not count, because a judge cannot read it. The value shown in a text field is counted, since it is on screen. A token counts as a word if it contains a letter or a digit, so “Adu / Raagu” counts as two and the separators count as none.

04

Against the grammar

Every rule is one the app already keeps somewhere; the question is which ones each candidate bends, and why. A bend needs a reason in one sentence. The numbers quoted are the ones measured on this page.

A — Two panes, stripped

Keeps

  • Rule 6 — the eight subtitles and the status words are gone; a tick is its own instruction.
  • Rule 3 — the tick is ink, not green. Nothing on this screen is a verdict about a recitation, so nothing on it is coloured.
  • Rule 8 — type and spacing come straight off the scale; nothing was nudged to make the two panes agree.
  • Rule 9 — every secondary line is --ink-3, which is the measured 5.35:1, not the old 2.76:1 grey.

Bends

  • Rule 1 — the rail is still a full pane of chrome beside the pane that holds the actual facts, and this is still the wordiest candidate.
  • Rule 2 — the criterion list lives under Judging panel and under Marks, and each has a Change link; two places look responsible for one decision.

B — Checklist

Keeps

  • Rule 1 — one column, no second pane; the facts get the room and there is no rail arguing with them.
  • Rule 2 — a task's controls open inside the line that names it, so there is exactly one place to point for each decision.
  • Rule 6 — a finished line is a mark and a value; it explains nothing, because there is nothing left to explain.
  • Rule 7 — the shortest of the three screens that show everything, so it is the least likely to push Start out of reach.

Bends

  • Rule 1 — two of the nine facts, the division list and the panel's coverage, are not on screen at rest; the collapse that buys the room also hides evidence.
  • Rule 10 — a collapsed line is 40px tall, under the 44px floor. Nothing here is pressed during a live recitation, which is the only reason that stands.

C — Progressive stepper

Keeps

  • Rule 1 — the fewest words of the four by a wide margin, and the task on screen owns the whole width.
  • Rule 7 — the shortest screen measured here, and no step of it reaches the 711px budget; the review step comes closest and still clears it.
  • Rule 5 — Start exists only on the last step, so no press early in the walk can begin a competition by accident.

Bends

  • Rule 4 — reaching Start costs eight presses rather than one deliberate gesture, and changing the marks costs four. This is the stepper the mark bar retired, wearing a different hat.
  • Rule 2 — a fact and the thing that changes it are never on screen together; checking the panel against the marks means walking between two steps and remembering.
  • Rule 6 — the strip has to be told what it is (“Step 1 of 8”), because eight small bars cannot say it themselves.

D — Dense summary

Keeps

  • Rule 2 — the row is the readout and the row is the control, exactly as the pinpointed criteria work on the Mushaf page.
  • Rule 6 — no Change links and no task list; the dashed rule around a value says what a sentence would have said.
  • Rule 1 — nine facts, nine rows, nothing between them and the reader.

Bends

  • Rule 5 — every value on the confirmation screen is one press from being edited, on the one screen whose job is to be checked rather than changed.
  • Rule 6 — the dashed rule is quieter than a word, but it is also less certain; a judge who has never seen it has to try one to learn it.
  • Rule 10 — the value controls are 32px tall, under the 44px floor, and the density that makes the screen fit is what keeps them there.
