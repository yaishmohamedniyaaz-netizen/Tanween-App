01

The problem

The board holds twenty numbers in five columns of four. Each tile measures 116×76px — a size chosen for a phone, in a dialog that only ever opens on the laptop the app actually runs on. At 1024×768, a common laptop resolution, the board pushed the dialog's footer out of reach: the rarely-used “use an external question” link sat below the fold with no scroller, which by the grammar's own test (rule 7) makes it unreachable rather than merely awkward.

The instruction text above the board has already been cut from 78 words to 59. A grid of twenty numbers needs very little explaining — rule 6 says a grid is its own instruction — but 59 words is still more than most of the candidates below need.

Every candidate that follows renders the same twenty numbers, with 3, 7 and 12 already taken, so the taken state and the overflow risk can both be seen rather than described.

04

Against the grammar

Every rule is one the app already keeps somewhere; the question is which ones each candidate bends, and why. Numbers below are the ones measured on this page.

A — Board as-is, tightened

Keeps

  • Rule 2 — the grid is the only control; there is no second place to point.
  • Rule 4 — one tap commits one number.
  • Rule 6 — the grid needs no instruction beyond itself.
  • Rule 10 — 110×62 clears the 44px floor on both sides.

Bends

  • Rule 1 — five fixed columns still ask for more width than a compact dialog wants to give a control that is not the evidence.
  • Rule 5 — a tap commits instantly, with no press-drag-release correction of the kind rule 4 elsewhere relies on; a mis-tap is a mis-pick.

B — Dense grid

Keeps

  • Rule 7 — the shortest board of the four, with the least chance of pushing anything below it out of reach.
  • Rule 8 — built entirely on the 4px grid, down to its 4px gaps.
  • Rule 10 — 64×52 still clears 44px on both sides, despite being the smallest tile here.

Bends

  • Rule 5 — ten columns with 4px between them is the least forgiving spacing of the four; a finger that lands 4px off lands on the wrong number, and the tap still commits at once.

C — Numeric keypad

Keeps

  • Rule 10 — 78×78 is the largest target of the four, well clear of the 44px floor.
  • Rule 6 — a keypad shape needs no legend; people already know how to read one.

Bends

  • Rule 7 — measured over the 420px budget on this page. The tile size that makes rule 10 easy is the same size that pushes the board past the region that caused the original overflow.
  • Rule 1 — the tallest control of the four takes the most room from a dialog that is meant to be chrome around a decision, not the decision's own monument.

D — Type the number

Keeps

  • Rule 7 — by far the shortest board; nothing here is at risk of overflow.
  • Rule 5 — the number sits visible in the input before Enter commits it, so a mis-typed digit is caught before it counts, unlike a tap that commits on contact.
  • Rule 1 — the smallest footprint of the four, leaving the most room for the rest of the dialog.

Bends

  • Rule 4 — picking #14 costs three presses, not the one deliberate gesture the rest of the app aims for.
  • Rule 10 — the fallback list's rows are 36px tall, short of the 44px floor; typing and pressing Enter is the primary path precisely because the list is a secondary one.
  • Rule 6 — a text field is less self-evident than a grid of numbers and needs the one line of hint it carries.
